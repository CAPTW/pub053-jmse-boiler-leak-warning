Feedwater-Demand Residuals for Early Tube-Leak Warning in a 
Closed-Loop Auxiliary-Boiler Simulation
Technical Brief — author-review revision R2, 16 September 2026. Author details and submission declarations are maintained separately.
Abstract
Closed-loop regulation can keep drum pressure near its 
setpoint while increasing the control effort needed to 
compensate for leakage. This study compares confirmed 
warning times of specified pressure/level, flow-imbalance 
and feedwater-demand detectors in a reduced auxiliary-
boiler simulation. Twelve operating blocks each comprised 
a normal and a tube-leak episode; four development-normal 
episodes supplied references, scales and thresholds, and 
eight further blocks supplied evaluation trajectories. The 
detectors used conditioned observations and applied 
commands, without a matched normal trajectory or future 
fault information. A post-hoc component analysis applied 
the same development-normal threshold-selection rule 
separately to the flow and demand components of the 
original combined score. All four variants confirmed a 
post-onset alarm in all eight evaluation fault episodes and 
before the retrospectively identified functional-degradation 
start in the seven event-observed episodes. Median strict 
lead times were 34.7 min for the specified state baseline, 
48.9 min for flow alone, and 59.0 min for demand alone 
and the combined score. Demand alone matched the 
combined first alarm in seven blocks and was 8 s later in 
one; flow alone was earlier in two blocks. During 45.3 h of 
normal evaluation monitoring, the state baseline issued one 
confirmed alarm and the other variants none. The results 
attribute most combined-score first-alarm timing to 
feedwater demand in these scenarios, not to a general 
benefit of fusion. They support a limited detector 
comparison, not intrinsic sensor superiority, field validation 
or remaining-life prediction accuracy.
Keywords: tube-leak monitoring; auxiliary boiler; closed-
loop simulation; control demand; component analysis; 
functional endpoint
1. Introduction
Boiler leakage can increase the replenishment and heat 
input needed to maintain operation before a large departure 
of the regulated pressure or level becomes apparent. The 
diagnostic significance of feedwater demand is not new. Yu 
et al. (2016) explicitly discuss increased make-up-water 
requirements and the ability of feedwater margin to 
accommodate small leaks. The question here is narrower: 
when alarms require sustained evidence, how do particular 
state, flow and demand detectors compare, and which 
component determines a combined detector’s first warning?
Earlier boiler studies provide stronger plant-based evidence 
than the present simulation. Sun, Chen, and Marquez 
(2002) combined process modelling and forgetting-factor 
least squares using simulated and plant data. Sun, Marquez, 
Chen, and Riaz (2005) studied a modified PCA approach to 
early detection and false alarms. Yu et al. (2016) used 
multivariate plant monitoring, and Indrawan et al. (2021) 
considered several multivariate methods on a commercial 
boiler. Widodo et al. (2025) subsequently examined data-
driven diagnosis and a separate remaining-life analysis 
using three boilers. We do not replicate these methods, 
compare their numerical lead times with ours, or claim to 
discover the general usefulness of control effort.
This brief instead measures a specific relationship within 
one declared feedback experiment: confirmed alarm timing 
of a normal-conditioned flow/demand score versus a 
specified pressure/level baseline, followed by attribution of 
that timing to its components. The result is an episode-level 
comparison against an explicitly defined functional-
degradation criterion, including normal alarms, one 
censored fault episode and a nonuniform component 
ordering. That comparison—not a new universal leak-
detection algorithm—is the intended contribution. Because 
the component analysis was added after inspection of the 
original combined-score results, it is exploratory reanalysis 
of the same evaluation trajectories.

2.2. Feedback, protection and block design
Pressure PI control determines fuel demand. A level PI 
contributes a trim to observed steam flow to form the 
feedwater-flow setpoint, and an inner PI drives the 
feedwater valve. A 60 s manual hot-start hold precedes 
cascade regulation. Controller and sensor periods are 2 s; 
the controller uses the previous step’s conditioned 
observations. Finite good-quality samples update each 
channel; invalid samples hold the last valid value for at 
most 10 s, after which the channel is unavailable. Sensor 
models include lag, noise, dropout and pressure spikes. 
These assumptions are fixed and listed in Supplement S2.
Process alarms, trips and lockout remain active but evaluate 
idealized plant states. Scheduler-only interventions based 
on known severity thresholds are excluded from actuation 
and retained as diagnostic labels. An inherited saturation-
timeout ambiguity remains unresolved. The experiment 
therefore does not represent fully sensor-based protection 
or validate every protection function. Plant truth is 
excluded from detector inputs but retained for this 
protection layer and retrospective endpoint annotation. No 
detector actuates the controller or protection logic.
Four development and eight evaluation blocks each have 
normal and tube-leak branches: 24 six-hour episodes and 
259,224 timestamps. Each split is balanced across the two 
base-load settings. Branches share a plant draw, physical-
time load realization and sensor innovations applied to their 
own states. They are paired branches, not independent 
replicates, and the normal counterpart is never an input to 
the fault-branch detector. Leak onset, progression duration 
and k were drawn within 2,700–5,400 s, 5,400–10,800 s 
and 0.16–0.24, respectively. The original D4 plant draw 
failed at furnace construction; its replacement retained the 
other input streams. Actual draws and the construction-
valid selection condition are reported in Supplement S2.
The final fitting routine consumed only D1–D4 normal 
records. Evaluation trajectories were generated before the 
final D4 replacement and fit were completed. Input binding 
and refitting establish the numerical development-only 
dependency, not an absence of every earlier informal 
inspection. We therefore describe an evaluation set rather 
than claiming that final artifacts were frozen before its 
generation. The added component comparison is explicitly 
post hoc.

alarm requires strict exceedance for 600 elapsed seconds

start. Unavailability resets persistence. The 1,800 s 
cooldown controls repeated notifications, which are not 
counted as new leaks.

3. Results
3.1. Physical response under compensation
Figure 1 places the E1 warning problem in its physical 
setting. After the 3,374 s leak onset, the pressure 
observation remains close to its setpoint, while applied 
feedwater command eventually rises well above the 
development-normal reference. The command approaches 
its available upper range as level declines toward the 
functional criterion. Figure S5 shows the accompanying 
feedwater and steam observations. This sequence is 
consistent with replenishment compensating liquid loss 
until the simulated feedwater/level margin is depleted; it is 
not evidence that level is perfectly unchanged before 
warning.
Figure 1. Recorded E1 tube-leak observations and applied feedwater 
command. The dotted vertical line is leak onset; the shaded band is 
functional dwell. The normal-conditioned command reference is fitted 
to development normals, not obtained from E1’s paired healthy 
trajectory. Panel axes differ. The level criterion is applied to plant

Demand and combined first alarms occur at 7,232 s, the 
specified B0 alarm at 8,996 s, and functional 
start/confirmation at 10,774/10,894 s. These are 
confirmations of different score processes, not the first 
physical departure of each signal. The normal evaluation 
branches retain regulated operation; full normal histories 
and the configured model envelope are supplied in the 
supplement.

3.3. Normal alarms and interpretation boundaries
During 45.33 h of normal evaluation monitoring, B0 issues 
one confirmed alarm in E8 and the other variants none. 
That same early B0 alarm occurs at 3,276 s in E8’s paired 
fault branch, before its 3,838 s onset; it is not another 
independent normal realization. For E8 combined B1, peak 
score 4.6167 exceeds threshold 4.3883, but the longest 
continuous exceedance is only 464 s. The highest minimum 
score over a full 600 s window is 4.2438. Persistence 
therefore prevents a confirmation on that observed path; 
this is not a certified robustness margin or a long-run rate 
bound (Figure S4).

4. Discussion
The observed timing is consistent with control 
compensation: feedwater demand rises as the controller 
attempts to offset liquid loss, while regulated pressure and 
delayed level excursions can yield later state-baseline 
alarms. This mechanism is already recognized in boiler 
monitoring; the added evidence here is the timing and 
component attribution under a specified feedback, 
persistence and endpoint policy. The comparison shows 
that the selected composite score’s earliest confirmation 
was usually demand-driven, with two exceptions favouring 
separately thresholded flow. It does not demonstrate 
universal fusion benefit or reproduce the field validation of 
earlier studies.
Three design features restrict that interpretation. First, 
filtering, reference adjustment and score sidedness differ 
across pipelines. The earlier demand alarms cannot be 
attributed solely to an inherently superior information 
source. Second, the functional criterion contains feedwater 
command, so a demand anomaly may track a variable used 
in the endpoint definition. This is not future-label leakage, 
but a different independent endpoint could change the 
utility assessment. Stored-trajectory level-cutoff 
sensitivities are exploratory and do not remove the shared 
command condition. Third, the component comparison 
retains the original sensor-availability policy. Removing a 
score is not the same as removing its instruments from a 
deployed system.
The experiment remains small and synthetic. Its two steam-
valve regimes, accelerated liquid-loss law, construction-
valid draws, idealized process protection and controller 
tuning bound the population studied. Pairing controls 
exogenous inputs but does not increase independent sample 
size; all variants also share development fitting and 
evaluation
 
trajectories.
 
Geometry-scaled 
circulation/relaxation parameters, fixed feedwater 
temperature, absent superheater and simplified drum 
inventory limit physical transfer. Normal-regression 
adjustment does not identify these omitted mechanisms. 
The reported zero alarms cover only the finite normal 
exposure, not sustained sensor failure or out-of-distribution 
operation.

These limits suggest a focused use of the result: a 
transparent reference comparison for studying how a 
controller redistributes observable fault effects. A future 
independent study should keep detector settings fixed while 
varying control and operating conditions, include an 
endpoint not defined partly by the demand signal, and test 
against independent operating evidence. Such work would 
extend, rather than be established by, the present detector 
comparison.
5. Conclusion
Within the specified simulated operating conditions and 
threshold-selection policy, the demand-residual detector 
issued earlier confirmed post-onset alarms than the 
specified pressure/level baseline in all eight evaluation fault 
episodes. Demand and combined scores had a 59.0 min 
median strict lead in seven event-observed episodes, versus 
34.7 min for B0. Demand alone retained the combined first-
alarm timing in seven blocks, but separately thresholded 
flow was earlier in two. The evidence therefore supports a 
limited pipeline and component comparison: it neither 
identifies the first physical state deviation nor proves 
intrinsic sensor superiority, general fusion benefit, 
remaining-life accuracy or field readiness. No confirmed 
flow, demand or combined alarm was observed during 45.3 
h of normal evaluation; that finite observation is not a 
reliability guarantee.
